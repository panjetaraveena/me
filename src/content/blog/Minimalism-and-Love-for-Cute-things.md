I've been <strong data-lexical-text="true">**surprised**</strong> by how difficult minimalism becomes the moment something has tiny ears and a smiling face.
<br><br>
I can ignore a sensible mug. I can walk past a practical storage box. But give either one rosy cheeks and little paws, and suddenly I'm standing in a shop trying to explain why this object is <em data-lexical-text="true">_different_</em>. It is not clutter. It is a small ceramic frog with an important job.

## Minimalism, meet cuteness
Minimalism is often presented as a serious commitment to owning less: neutral rooms, empty shelves, and one extremely responsible chair. I like the idea of owning less. Fewer things mean fewer things to clean, organize, move, repair, and feel vaguely guilty about. But I also love objects that are playful, comforting, and unnecessarily adorable. For a while, these preferences seemed incompatible. Either I could be a disciplined minimalist or I could own a collection of tiny animal-shaped things. Apparently, adulthood required a choice.

It doesn't.

## Keep the delight
Minimalism does not have to mean removing everything without a practical function. It can mean removing the things that no longer serve a purpose—including the purpose of making ordinary life feel a little more delightful. A cute object can earn its place. The tiny bear lamp can provide light. The smiling bowl can hold breakfast. The ridiculous duck-shaped pouch can store cables while looking deeply pleased with itself.

The rule I've settled on is simple: <strong data-lexical-text="true">**cute things must be chosen, not accumulated.**</strong>
I don't need every charming object I encounter. I only need the few that I genuinely enjoy, use, or would notice if they disappeared. Minimalism helps me create space. Cute things make that space feel like mine.

Somehow, the tiny ceramic frog stays.
